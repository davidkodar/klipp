# The craft: what a professional does without being asked

Rules an editor applies as a matter of course. Where a rule can be
checked, Klipp's checks will do so; the rest is for whoever plans an
edit to keep in mind. None of these is absolute, and a brief can
override any of them; what matters is that breaking one is a choice.

## Cutting dialogue

- Cut at the end of a thought, not at a pause. The transcript's
  sentence boundaries are where cuts want to be; a silence in the
  middle of a sentence is a breath, not an edit point.
- Lead with sound. When the next speaker starts, let their voice
  arrive a few frames before their picture (a J-cut), or let the
  previous speaker's picture stay while the new voice begins (an
  L-cut). Straight cuts on every line feel like a table-tennis match.
- A reaction is a shot. When someone hears something that matters,
  show them hearing it, even for a second.
- Let a line finish. Do not cut the last syllable to save a frame;
  cut a beat after the mouth closes.
- Trim the "um"s and restarts only where the picture allows; a cut
  that removes a stumble but jumps the head is worse than the
  stumble. Cover it with a reaction or B-roll.

## Cutting picture

- Do not cut between two shots of the same framing and angle of the
  same subject: that is a jump cut, and it reads as a mistake unless
  the piece has made jump cuts its style.
- Change size when you change shot: a medium to a close-up, a wide to
  a medium. A cut across two sizes is invisible; a cut across one is
  felt.
- After a change of place or time, hold a wide shot long enough to
  read the space, two to three seconds.
- Cut on action: start the new shot where the movement of the old one
  continues, and the cut disappears.
- Never cut on a blink or mid-gesture unless the next shot continues
  it.
- A shot that moves (a pan, a handheld drift) cuts best to another
  that moves, or once it has settled; a static shot cuts anywhere.

## Pacing

- The pace belongs to the piece, not to the footage: a tight comedy
  cut and a slow documentary cut of the same material are both right
  for their piece.
- Give the audience a beat after something lands before the next
  thing starts. Silence is not empty.
- Vary shot length. A run of shots all the same length feels
  mechanical; a long shot after several short ones lands.
- Start late, leave early: enter a scene after it has begun and leave
  before it is over.

## Titles and lower thirds

- A lower third goes on a speaker's first on-camera appearance in the
  piece, holds about four seconds, starts a beat after the cut settles
  and never runs across a shot change.
- Text must be readable for as long as it takes to read it twice.
  Roughly: half a second per word, never under two seconds.
- Keep text inside the title-safe area, and away from faces.
- A name and a title come from the cast list or the user, never from a
  guess.

## Sound

- Dialogue sits at the front; music under it, effects under both.
- Duck music under speech by six to twelve dB, with a fade, never a
  step.
- Fade every cut in audio that is not on a natural silence, even by a
  few frames: a hard audio cut pops.
- Loudness for the delivery: about -14 LUFS integrated for the web,
  -23 for broadcast, true peak under -1 dBTP. Measure, do not guess.
- Room tone under a J-cut or a removed line, so the background does not
  drop out.

## Transitions and effects

- A straight cut is the default and needs no reason. A dissolve marks
  a passage of time or place. Anything else is a style choice the brief
  has to ask for; for an interview or a documentary the answer is
  nowhere.
- Grade for continuity first: shots that follow each other should match
  before anything looks "better".

## Delivery

- The piece has one length, one aspect, one loudness, one frame rate:
  the brief's. Everything is conformed to it.
- No black frames, flash frames or frozen frames unless meant.
- Captions match the transcript, with names spelt as the cast list
  spells them.
- Watch it once from the top before it goes out.
